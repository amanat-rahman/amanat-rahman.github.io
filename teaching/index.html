Teaching philosophy

Six years as a full-time faculty member in the Industrial and Production Engineering discipline at Ahsanullah University of Science and Technology, carrying a full load of theory courses and laboratories each semester, shaped four principles that guide course design.

Introduce each method through the decision it supports. Linear programming enters through a production mix problem: a plant with limited machine hours must choose how much of each product to make, and students propose plans by hand before the constraints and objective are written down, so the simplex method arrives as the answer to a question they have already asked. Production planning begins from a schedule that ran out of stock, and only then defines the inventory balance constraints that would have prevented it. Students who meet the decision before the method ask more precise questions about it, because they already know what its answer has to do.

Design the course for every student who enrolls in it. Most students take operations research, production planning, engineering economy, or numerical methods because the curriculum requires them, arriving with different preparation and different degrees of interest. Each course starts from the prerequisite knowledge students share, and each new topic names the prerequisite it draws on so a student who needs to review knows what to review. Learning objectives open each topic, worked examples precede any abstraction, and assessments test those objectives directly, so a student who loses marks can identify the objective not yet met rather than concluding the subject is beyond them.

Use laboratories to make abstraction testable. A laboratory lets a student see the consequence of an error directly: a simulation whose output distribution is implausible, or a formed part outside tolerance, gives feedback a graded problem set cannot. Laboratory work is sequenced so the first error occurs early, when it is inexpensive to correct, and so diagnosing it requires the concept from the preceding lecture.

Fix the objectives and assessments; adapt the delivery. Teaching in two education systems that differ in class size, assessment culture, and software access required separating what students must learn from how they are best supported in learning it. Course content stays fixed; the amount of guided practice, the pace of derivations, and the balance between hand computation and solver work change to fit the students in the room.

University of Cincinnati, 2026

Recitation instructor, Deterministic Systems Models (ISE 3011), the undergraduate operations research course, covering linear programming formulation, the simplex method, sensitivity analysis, and network models. Teaching this material to a cohort with full solver access showed how much modeling intuition students gain from perturbing a model and observing how the solution changes, an exercise now built into every optimization course.

University of Louisville, 2022

Teaching assistant and recitation instructor, Engineering Economic Analysis (IE 370). Delivered review lectures, held office hours, and graded examinations.

Curriculum development

Authored the laboratory manuals for four courses: Industrial Simulation, Manufacturing Process, and Machine Shop Practice I and II. All four were adopted as standing departmental materials and remained in use across subsequent cohorts. Manuals have to work for any instructor who teaches from them, so writing them meant specifying learning objectives, sequencing exercises around the equipment the department owned, and building grading rubrics that different instructors apply the same way.

Contributed to curriculum revision through outcome assessment and course mapping in support of program accreditation, tracing individual assignments back to program-level outcomes and identifying where the curriculum promised coverage no course delivered.

Mentoring

Supervised five undergraduate final-year project and thesis groups from problem formulation through data collection, analysis, and defense. The most common difficulty in that work is scope: students propose problems larger than the time available, and much of the supervisor’s work is helping them define a version that is theirs, that they can finish, and that still makes a contribution.

Courses prepared to teach

Deterministic and probabilistic operations research, optimization and mathematical programming, stochastic models, discrete-event simulation, engineering economy, production planning and control, supply chain management, and engineering statistics and design of experiments. Also introductory programming and data analysis for engineers, and manufacturing processes and laboratory sequences.

Proposed undergraduate elective: Optimization Modeling for Engineering Decisions. A project-based course in which students formulate, solve, and defend an optimization model for a decision problem they select, working in solver environments from the first week. The emphasis falls on formulation and on interpreting a solution for a non-technical stakeholder, with the modeling cycle from problem to recommendation as the object of study.

Proposed graduate elective: Stochastic Programming and Decomposition Methods. Two-stage and multi-stage formulations, scenario generation and reduction, the L-shaped method and its integer extension, and Benders decomposition, taught through implementation. Students build a working decomposition solver over the term.
